## q004 (en)

**Q:** What lifestyle factors increase the risk of colorectal cancer?

**Reference:** Risk factors include excess body weight, physical inactivity, smoking, high consumption of red or processed meat, low calcium intake, low intake of fruits and vegetables, and heavy alcohol consumption.

## q014 (en)

**Q:** What are common causes of weight loss in bowel cancer patients?

**Reference:** Weight loss may be caused by the cancer itself, treatment side effects, appetite loss, diarrhoea, constipation, fatigue, nausea, or problems absorbing nutrients.

## q041 (en)

**Q:** What quality of life issues do colorectal cancer survivors commonly experience?

**Reference:** Colorectal cancer survivors may experience lasting side effects from treatment including bowel changes, fatigue, sexual dysfunction, emotional distress, and concerns about cancer recurrence. Survivorship care plans and support services can help address these issues.

## q046 (en)

**Q:** What emotional impact can having a stoma have and how can patients cope?

**Reference:** A stoma can affect a person's body image, self-confidence, and emotional wellbeing. Patients may feel anxious, low, or embarrassed. Talking to a stoma nurse, counsellor, or peer support group can help with emotional adjustment.

## q064 (en)

**Q:** Are cancer patients entitled to free prescriptions in the UK?

**Reference:** Yes, people with cancer are entitled to free NHS prescriptions in England if they have a medical exemption certificate. Prescriptions are already free in Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland regardless of diagnosis.

## q065 (en)

**Q:** Can bowel cancer patients apply for a Blue Badge parking permit?

**Reference:** Some bowel cancer patients may qualify for a Blue Badge if their condition significantly affects their ability to walk or if they are in severe pain when moving. The application is assessed individually and a diagnosis of cancer alone does not automatically qualify someone.

## q068 (en)

**Q:** How does the large bowel work normally?

**Reference:** The large bowel, or colon, absorbs water and salts from undigested food, turning liquid waste from the small intestine into solid stools. Stools are stored in the rectum and passed out through the anus when the person is ready to open their bowels.

## q083 (en)

**Q:** What bowel changes can occur after colon surgery and how long do they last?

**Reference:** After colon surgery, patients may have looser stools, more frequent bowel movements, urgency, or difficulty controlling the bowel. These changes often improve gradually over weeks to months as the bowel adapts. Diet, pelvic floor exercises, and medications can help manage symptoms.

## q087 (en)

**Q:** Which immunotherapy drugs are used for dMMR metastatic colon cancer?

**Reference:** For dMMR (mismatch repair deficient) or MSI-H metastatic colon cancer, immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab (Keytruda) and nivolumab (Opdivo), with or without ipilimumab, are approved treatments. dMMR tumours tend to respond particularly well to immunotherapy.

## q095 (en)

**Q:** How should a clean-catch urine specimen be collected from a urostomy stoma?

**Reference:** To collect a clean-catch specimen, remove the pouch, clean the stoma with sterile gauze, allow a small amount of urine to flow and discard it, then collect the mid-stream specimen directly into a sterile container, taking care not to touch the stoma with the container.

## q110 (en)

**Q:** What follow-up is recommended after curative resection of colorectal cancer?

**Reference:** After curative resection, follow-up typically includes regular clinical consultations, CEA blood tests every three to six months for the first three years, CT scans every six to twelve months for the first three years, and colonoscopy at one year post-surgery. The schedule may vary by guideline and individual risk.

## q114 (en)

**Q:** What does life look like after treatment for bowel cancer?

**Reference:** Life after bowel cancer treatment may involve adjusting to changes in bowel function, managing fatigue, attending regular follow-up appointments, and making lifestyle changes. Many people return to full activity and enjoy a good quality of life after treatment, though some experience lasting side effects.

## q127 (en)

**Q:** What health inequalities exist in bowel cancer outcomes?

**Reference:** Health inequalities in bowel cancer include worse outcomes for people living in deprived areas, those from minority ethnic groups, people with learning disabilities, and older adults. These groups are less likely to be screened, diagnosed at an earlier stage, or receive guideline-recommended treatment.

## q177 (en)

**Q:** What symptoms should prompt urgent re-referral after colorectal cancer treatment?

**Reference:** Symptoms warranting urgent re-referral include rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, new or worsening abdominal pain, change in bowel habit, rising CEA levels, new symptoms suggesting distant metastasis such as persistent cough or bone pain, and any other concerns about possible recurrence.

## q178 (en)

**Q:** How frequently should CEA be checked during colorectal cancer follow-up?

**Reference:** CEA should be measured every three to six months for the first three years after curative treatment for colorectal cancer. A rising CEA level can indicate recurrence before imaging detects it and should prompt further investigation and review.

## q205 (en)

**Q:** How can dehydration be managed and prevented with an ileostomy?

**Reference:** People with an ileostomy are at higher risk of dehydration because the colon, which absorbs water, has been bypassed. Patients should drink at least two litres of fluid per day, increase intake in hot weather or during exercise, replace electrolytes (especially sodium and potassium), and recognise signs of dehydration such as dark urine, thirst, and fatigue.

## q211 (en)

**Q:** What should new ostomy patients expect in the first weeks after surgery?

**Reference:** In the first weeks, new ostomy patients are learning to manage their pouching system with support from a stoma nurse. The stoma may be swollen initially and will shrink over four to eight weeks. Output will vary and stabilise over time. Emotions may fluctuate and this is normal. Practical support and information are key at this stage.

## q214 (en)

**Q:** What resources are available to support new ostomy patients?

**Reference:** Resources include specialist stoma care nurses, home delivery services for pouching supplies, patient organisations such as UOAA and the Colostomy Association, online communities and forums, printed patient education materials, and peer visitor programmes where people can speak with experienced ostomy patients.

## vi001 (vi)

**Q:** Hậu môn nhân tạo là gì và tại sao tôi cần làm phẫu thuật này?

**Reference:** A stoma is a surgically created opening in the abdomen through which stool leaves the body into a pouch, used when part of the bowel needs to be bypassed or rested after surgery for bowel cancer.

## vi002 (vi)

**Q:** Làm sao để biết vết mổ của tôi bị nhiễm trùng?

**Reference:** Signs of wound infection include increasing redness spreading from the wound, swelling, warmth, increasing pain, pus or discharge, a bad smell, and fever. These require prompt medical attention.

## vi003 (vi)

**Q:** Tôi có thể tắm khi đang mang túi hậu môn nhân tạo không?

**Reference:** Yes. You can shower with the pouch on or off; water does not harm the stoma. Avoid pointing the shower directly at the stoma and dry the skin and the pouch adhesive thoroughly afterwards.

## vi004 (vi)

**Q:** Sau phẫu thuật ung thư đại trực tràng tôi nên ăn gì và kiêng gì?

**Reference:** Eat small frequent meals, chew thoroughly, and reintroduce fibre gradually. In the early weeks limit high-fibre, gassy, and very fatty foods, and drink enough fluid. Dietary advice should be individualised with the care team.

## vi005 (vi)

**Q:** Da quanh lỗ hậu môn nhân tạo của tôi bị đỏ và rát, tôi phải làm sao?

**Reference:** Sore peristomal skin is usually caused by stool leaking under the adhesive or a poorly fitting appliance. Check the pouch aperture matches the stoma size, ensure the skin is clean and fully dry before applying, and contact the stoma nurse if it does not settle.

## vi006 (vi)

**Q:** Khi nào tôi cần đi cấp cứu sau mổ ung thư đại trực tràng?

**Reference:** Seek urgent care for severe or worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, no stoma output with a swollen abdomen, heavy bleeding, high fever, or a wound that opens. These can indicate obstruction, infection or a leak.

## vi007 (vi)

**Q:** Đóng hậu môn nhân tạo là gì và quá trình hồi phục ra sao?

**Reference:** Stoma reversal is an operation that rejoins the bowel so stool passes normally again. Bowel habit is often unpredictable for weeks to months afterwards, with frequency and urgency that usually improve over time.

## vi008 (vi)

**Q:** Tôi bị táo bón sau phẫu thuật, có nên dùng thuốc nhuận tràng không?

**Reference:** Constipation after surgery is common and is usually managed first with fluids, gentle activity and dietary fibre. Any laxative should be discussed with the care team, since some are unsuitable after bowel surgery.

## vi009 (vi)

**Q:** Tôi nên thay túi hậu môn nhân tạo bao lâu một lần?

**Reference:** A drainable pouch is typically emptied when about one third full and changed every one to three days; a closed pouch is changed when it is about one third full. The exact routine depends on the appliance and the individual.

## vi010 (vi)

**Q:** Ung thư đại trực tràng có những dấu hiệu sớm nào?

**Reference:** Early signs include rectal bleeding or blood in the stool, a persistent change in bowel habit, abdominal pain or cramping, a feeling the bowel does not empty completely, unexplained weight loss, and fatigue from anaemia.

## vi011 (vi)

**Q:** Tôi có thể tập thể dục trở lại sau bao lâu kể từ khi mổ?

**Reference:** Light walking usually starts within days. Heavy lifting and strenuous exercise are generally avoided for around six weeks to reduce the risk of hernia, and activity is increased gradually on the surgical team's advice.

## vi012 (vi)

**Q:** Tôi thấy lo lắng và buồn bã sau khi mổ, điều đó có bình thường không?

**Reference:** Anxiety and low mood are common after cancer surgery and a change in body image. Support is available through the clinical team, stoma nurses and patient organisations, and persistent symptoms should be raised with a healthcare professional.
